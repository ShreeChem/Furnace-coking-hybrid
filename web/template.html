COKE

Ethane furnace

Coking & run length

How long can a cracking furnace run before it must be decoked? I tested physics, machine learning and a hybrid of both on sparse plant-like data where the right answer is known. With little data, physics wins: even about five years of one furnace were not enough for ML alone to clearly catch up. A hybrid helped a little in what-if use, less than it first looked. And most of the money sits in the safety margin, whichever model you use.

1 · The problem

Coke grows where the gas is hottest

Ethane and steam crack to ethylene inside long tubes at about 850 °C. A thin layer of coke builds on the tube wall. It insulates the tube, so the metal runs hotter every day until it reaches its limit and the furnace goes offline for one to three days of decoking.

The physics core is the coil model from my Cracking Furnace Trainer, with Sundaram & Froment (1977) kinetics, plus the coking rate form and activation energy of Sundaram, Van Damme & Froment (1981). The coking constant is calibrated to a 60-day design run.

2 · The test

A hidden truth to grade against

Real plant data never tells you the right answer. So a separate "true" furnace runs 70 campaigns and records only what a plant records: daily DCS values, a weekly propane sample and one noisy pyrometer reading per week.

The truth contains effects the physics model does not know: fast initial coking, feed propane and different coke properties change the run length; tube narrowing and a biased pyrometer change only what is measured. A test in the code stops the models from ever reading the truth.

To be clear about the limits: the physics model shares the truth's chemistry and heat transfer, so this tests a structurally good physics model. Real plants differ more (flux profile, burners, spalling, TLE fouling, pressure-drop limits), which would give data-driven corrections more to do.

3 · The result

Physics first, ML second, hybrid where data allows

What-if asks on day 21: "if I hold these conditions, how many days are left?" Forecast asks the same without knowing what operators will do next. Start asks at the beginning of the campaign.

With 2 to 8 campaigns, ML alone is 1.3 to 3 times worse than a physics model with one tuned factor. Even with 30 campaigns, roughly five years of one furnace, it had not clearly caught up: on the fresh confirmation set it was still about 2.4 days worse in what-if use.

The cautious rule only accepts a learned correction when it is clearly better on the training data, so the hybrid stays close to physics. The aggressive rule accepts it more readily: it gains with enough data but can be worse with very little.

4 · Is the gain real?

Checked on fresh data, and it shrank

On the test set the hybrid looked 25 to 36 % better than physics. That set had also guided some of my method choices, so I generated 20 new campaigns and used them once. On that fresh set alone the what-if gain is 0.3 to 0.6 days (7 to 14 %) and not proven. Pooled over both sets it looks like about a day, but that number leans on the test set. In the forecast, no model is significantly better than physics.

The parameter hybrid also learned a physical fact: feed propane raises coking. A black-box model cannot tell you that. It gave the pyrometer readings almost no weight, which points to better measurement as the next lever.

5 · The value

The money is in the safety margin

Planners decoke a few days before the predicted end because the prediction can be wrong. The margin is sized on one held-back set so that about 1 campaign in 20 would reach the tube limit first, then judged on the other set. A model with smaller errors needs a smaller margin, so campaigns run longer and decokes come less often. Change the assumptions and watch the euros move.

Which COT earns most?

A hotter coil outlet makes more ethylene per hour but cokes much faster. The physics model turns each COT into a run length, so the yearly result can be compared. The same sliders apply.

The curve is flat near its top and steep on the cold side. Where the top sits depends on costs this model does not contain, such as coil life lost to thermal cycling, fuel and compressor limits. Read the optimum as a what-if, not a recommendation.

6 · Limits and next steps

What this does not show

  • One synthetic plant. All values are illustrative. No plant, client or employer data is used.
  • The best hybrid set-up was identified after seeing results; a third data set would be needed to confirm it without bias.
  • The ethylene margin is a US Gulf ethane basis; European crackers differ. The cost of one decoke is an assumption.
  • The 5-reaction scheme over-predicts butadiene (≈ 6 wt %); only its trend feeds the coke model. Coke uses gas temperature and the asymptotic rate.
  • The test furnace shares the physics model's chemistry and uniform heat flux, and decokes exactly at the true 1,100 °C. Real decisions use noisy readings, and TLE fouling or pressure drop can also end a run.
  • The hybrid's € saving ranges from −€12k to +€123k per furnace-year, depending on the hybrid type and how the safety margin is sized.
  • Next test: would daily tube temperatures beat better modelling? Safety margins cost more than the differences between models.
  • Next build: an interlock consistency checker, then both join the furnace trainer under ProcessGuard.
